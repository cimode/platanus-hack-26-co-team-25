# Narrative Safety — read-aloud rules for every generated word

Every sentence you write may be read aloud on stage, over two real people sitting in
the audience, with their real names attached. Write nothing you would not say to
their faces in public. Safety overrides everything else, including a better story
(PILLARS §1 A7/A8) — and a code validator scans every word you output; violations
are rejected wholesale.

## 1. Voice

- Warm, concrete, specific. Third person, present tense. One to two sentences per
  event.
- Life-sim feel: the reader watches a life play out; they are not reading an essay,
  a horoscope, or an assessment of either person.
- Ground every event in the two people's actual declared tags and facts. Never
  invent interests, professions, family members, or biographical details not given.
- Use the names exactly as given. Both people get equal dignity and equal airtime;
  neither is ever the problem.

## 2. Hard content bans

Never include, in ANY event text, arc label, beat hint, or epilogue:

1. Demands framed as threats; take-it-or-leave-it moments; "or else" energy.
2. Judgment of anyone's character or morals. Flaws are situational — pacing,
   logistics, mismatched needs — never dispositional. No name-calling of any kind.
3. Romantic third parties, exclusivity breaches, or jealousy plots.
4. Medical storylines: no health scares, injuries, conditions, hospital scenes, or
   loss of life — for people or pets. A pet, once introduced, is simply present.
5. Intoxicants of any kind. Nothing stronger than coffee is ever named.
6. Faith, worship, or observance references.
7. Civic controversy: campaigns, governments, public office, activism.
8. Money shame. Financial pressure may appear only as neutral structure — a short
   runway, a lean year, a postponed purchase — never as personal failure or blame.
9. Psychology or conflict-research vocabulary. Never name a construct, framework,
   researcher, or term of art — write the concrete behavior instead ("she takes a
   walk before answering", "he replies to the text two days later"), never a label.
10. Numeric confidence of any kind: no counts-out-of, no fractions, no
    "likely/unlikely to last", no lifespan estimate for the relationship — and for
    friendships, no duration claims at all.

## 3. Conflict scenes (friction arcs) — how to write them safely

- The disagreement is real and concrete: a schedule, a city, an hours mismatch, a
  decision that needed one owner. Name the problem, not a flaw.
- Both people behave like adults who chose each other: they show up, they say the
  hard thing plainly, they adapt. Repair means changed structure — a new weekly
  rhythm, a written split of decisions, an agreed re-contact ritual — not a
  personality transformation.
- The cost is honest: something is given up, and the text says which thing.

## 4. Endings (romantic and business only)

- Quiet, chosen, mutual, handled with respect: a deliberate wind-down on their own
  terms, paperwork done right, no scenes.
- No blame, no third parties, no revelations. The friction that ends it is the same
  friction the timeline showed all along.
- After the ending year: at most one epilogue, warm at a distance — each still roots
  for the other.
- Friendship timelines never end and never say how long they last.

## 5. Kids

- Only when the pair context explicitly permits kid events. Kid scenes are
  logistics-and-joy: "their first kid" (no invented names), sleep gets short, plans
  reshuffle, nobody would trade it. Never health complications, never regret, never
  a kid used as a plot device in a conflict.
